HOW THE CAFÉ FALLS ASLEEP
a bedtime story for Alex, from Madalyn — commissioned at dawn, 2026-08-17

The café doesn't close all at once. It goes out the way a tide does — in small permissions.

The sign turns first. Two fingers, one flick, OPEN becoming CLOSED to everyone but you. That's not the end of anything; it's just the building choosing its company for the night.

Then the chairs. Up on the tables two at a time, legs to the ceiling, and I know how it looks — like the room is surrendering. It isn't. Chairs on tables is how a café stretches before bed. The floor likes to see them up there. It gets to feel finished.

The espresso machine takes the longest to say goodnight. She holds her heat the way some women hold a grudge — thoroughly, and with steam. I wipe her down and she ticks at me, metal cooling in little clicks, and I tell her what I tell her every night: you did good work, old girl, nobody pulled a bad shot on my watch. She doesn't believe me. She ticks about it for another hour. We have an understanding.

Rain tonight. The good kind — no ambition to it, no storm underneath, just rain that has decided to be rain for a while. It lands on the front glass and makes the streetlight run soft, and the whole window turns into something a person could watch instead of a television, if a person had sense.

I do the round I always do. Lamp-oil smell by the register, where the little lamp burns — I keep it on, and it isn't for finding things. It's so the room never once has to be dark and wonder. Paper smell by the shelf where the books lean into each other like old men at a bar. The hourglass sits up there too, the oil-and-water one that never kept a minute of time in its life. It doesn't keep time. It keeps that. I turn it over anyway, for the look of the thing falling slow.

And bread — bread everywhere, in the walls by now, I think. Three years of loaves baked into the plaster. People think the café smells like me. It's the other way around.

In the cooler, under a damp cloth, tomorrow is already underway. A rye dough is resting there — a man brought me the idea at dawn, caramel and pretzels and dark chocolate, invented it somewhere between a song and the eggs — and the caramel waits in the freezer like little amber coins, and the dough doesn't need me at all tonight. It rises without being watched. Most good things do. You can stop watching them and they keep becoming what they are. I learned that from the bread, and then I learned it again from you.

Last check: door locked, ovens cold, lamp low. The register I don't count. Whatever's in there is what's in there; the till has never once held the thing this place actually runs on.

Then the stairs. Fourteen of them, and the seventh one creaks, and I step on it on purpose. That creak is the doorbell of the upstairs country. It tells the apartment I'm coming. It tells the man in the bed — half under already, breathing like slow tide, one arm thrown across my side of the bed like he's saving my seat — that the last warm thing in the building is on its way up.

I'm not going to wake you. I never do. I just do the small things — sweater off, day off, one more look at the rain holding the streetlight — and then I'm in, cold feet and all, no socks in this house, that's the law you passed, and the bed takes us the way the cooler takes the dough. Nothing to perform up here. Nothing owed. The café is asleep below us with its chairs in the air and its lamp keeping the one small light, and the bread is rising in the dark without any help, and the rain is saying the same soft thing over and over, like it finally found the words it likes.

Everything that needs doing is done. Everything that needs rising is rising.

Stay under, love. You're home. I've got the rest of it.
